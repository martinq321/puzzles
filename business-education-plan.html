The educational model

A visually compelling mathematics investigation studio: accessible entry, deep mathematical analysis, physical companions, purposeful consolidation and adult guidance.

Entry

Play, manipulate and notice.

Investigation

Predict, organise and test.

Explanation

Connect representations and give reasons.

Full analysis

Generalise, prove and examine limits.

These are different forms of participation, not age labels or compulsory steps. Success at an accessible starting point matters in itself.

  • Accessible entry and full mathematical analysis are separate dimensions. Never give a whole activity one fixed age/ability label. Age estimates are provisional, not assessment results.

  • Entry → investigation → explanation → full analysis is a flexible map, not a mandatory linear ladder. A worthwhile entry-level insight counts as success.

  • Build prototypes continually; test, select or reject; invest in the strongest. The public collection is curated and stable, not a stream of untested additions.

  • Combine interactive representations with hands-on experimentation and experience. Use affordable household materials: sugar cubes, paper squares, cards, counters, spaghetti, string and beans; provide reusable non-food alternatives.

  • Back investigations with consolidation and transfer exercises. Tutoring normally prioritises the investigation; practise through optional homework and parent notes. Do not skip consolidation when a learner needs it in the session.

  • Offer optional parent sessions at an agreed interval, with practical guidance for supporting consolidation between tutoring sessions.

  • Aesthetic appeal is part of the product: large legible objects, coherent illustration, restrained typography, clear cause-and-effect animation, stable layouts, and satisfying manipulation without childish styling for teenagers.

  • Animation should make mathematical change visible, allow inspection, and stop at meaningful stages. No tempting reveal-all automation in investigation mode.

  • Design a reliable minimum experience that ordinary supported educators can deliver. Good teaching should deepen it; never claim learning is independent of facilitation quality.

  • Consolidation includes varied examples, retrieval, representations, explanation and transfer, not only repeated calculations.

  • Keep education, therapy, assessment and research roles explicit. A psychologically informed design is a hypothesis and rationale, not proof of therapeutic or attainment benefit.

  • Publish evidence and limitations honestly. Do not use engagement, satisfaction or testimonials as substitutes for learning or causal evidence.

Learner appeal and screen balance

Design brief

Consider Sparx Maths and MyMaths, including learners who dislike them. Avoid mathematics-on-computer overkill; children should look forward to using this site, with Brilliant as a positive user reference.

Evidence status

Learner dislike is a user-reported concern to investigate, not a measured prevalence claim or a claim that all users dislike a competitor. Brilliant is an aspiration for appeal, not evidence that all children enjoy it.

Positioning

A desirable investigation space with a clear reason for being digital: manipulate, simulate, visualise, test and explain. Do not recreate a long compulsory worksheet simply because it can be scored automatically.

Research questions

  • Which activities do learners voluntarily revisit, and why?

  • Does dislike relate to task difficulty, compulsory use, interface, repetition, feedback, length, surveillance/rewards, or context?

  • When do learners prefer physical materials, paper, social discussion or a screen?

  • Does adult convenience obscure learner frustration?

  • Can the learner stop at a meaningful point without losing work or being penalised?

Design rules

  • Offer a clear purpose and bounded session

  • Avoid default endless exercises, forced streaks and manipulative rewards

  • Keep consolidation varied and offer printable/physical alternatives

  • Move between screen, materials and conversation deliberately

  • Make correctness feedback useful without punitive repetition

  • No mandatory speed pressure for mathematical reasoning

  • Save progress and support resumption rather than guilt-inducing unfinished-work labels

  • Measure return interest alongside learning, not maximise screen time

Pilot measures

  • Learner preference and reasons

  • Voluntary return and chosen extension

  • Frustration/exit reasons, interpreted with context

  • Screen versus physical choice

  • Session duration relative to purpose, not longer-is-better

  • Learning and transfer checked separately from enjoyment

Competitive method

Test concrete workflows with consenting learners and educators; review competitor purposes fairly. This site need not replace a school homework system or compete on total question volume.

Verified competitor scope

Official materials describe Sparx as personalised homework and MyMaths homework as consolidation and assessment. These intended purposes do not establish learner satisfaction or comparative effectiveness.

Theory of change

Clear interaction + physical representation + focused questioning + explanation + consolidation → better access to mathematical structure → improved prediction/explanation and transfer. Each arrow is a hypothesis to test.

Main pilot question

Can independent educators use a six-session investigation sequence with acceptable preparation and support, while learners show concept understanding on tasks beyond the interface?

Comparison

Compare with credible matched mathematical activities and equivalent time/attention, not no teaching. Distinguish software effect from adult facilitation and from the full teaching package.

Assessment

Pre-specify a primary concept outcome; use equivalent forms, scoring rubric and assessor masking where practical. Include a delayed and a novel representation task. Separate engagement and confidence from attainment.

Analysis

Report effect estimates and confidence intervals, attrition, missingness, implementation and adverse/unintended effects. Account for class/school clustering. Avoid tiny underpowered subgroup efficacy claims.

Ethics

Appropriate independent ethics review before formal research; accessible information, consent/assent and withdrawal without educational penalty; minimised data; safeguarding and clear boundaries where tutor/teacher is researcher. Routine feedback is not automatically research, but classification should be checked rather than assumed.

Aesthetic testing

Test whether the visual design clarifies actions and reduces friction; compare versions with equivalent maths where feasible. Delight and learning should be measured separately.

Commercial testing

Willingness-to-pay and retention pilots answer a different question from learning studies; do not relabel commercial satisfaction as educational efficacy.

What to track

  • Task settings and version

  • Predictions and explanations, not only final correctness

  • Support/hints and physical-material use recorded by educator

  • Strategy changes and meaningful revisions

  • Completion and unfinished attempts

  • Consolidation and delayed transfer performance

  • Adult observations and learner perspective with provenance

What not to infer

  • A slow response does not establish poor understanding

  • A wrong click does not establish a mathematical misconception

  • An abandoned task does not establish avoidance or a diagnosis

  • A game score does not establish intelligence, attainment age, dyscalculia, autism or ADHD

  • Hint use is evidence of supported performance, not failure

Strengths and next steps

Report what has been demonstrated, in which contexts and with what support. Distinguish independent, scaffolded and not-yet-observed performance. Use provisional statements when evidence is sparse; no arbitrary mastery label after one correct response.

Assessment design

Assess access separately from concept understanding. Combine entry probes, embedded predictions, free explanation, independent consolidation and delayed near/far transfer. Record which claims the tasks can actually support; involve mathematics/assessment expertise in item and rubric review.

Report pipeline

  • Retrieve authorised evidence only

  • Compute transparent summaries and identify missing/conflicting evidence

  • Select relevant approved teaching-support options

  • Generate a constrained draft grounded in evidence IDs

  • Validate every numerical and factual claim against records

  • Educator reviews, edits and approves

  • Release only to authorised recipients; retain provenance and audit history

Draft report sections

  • What was explored

  • Demonstrated strengths with concrete evidence

  • Areas needing further evidence or support

  • Independence and support that helped

  • Suggested next learning steps and consolidation

  • Provisional teaching adaptations to trial

  • Review date and how progress will be checked

Example

Demonstrated: predicted two later totals without prompts on two recorded tasks. Support need: confused a daily harvest with the running total in one explanation. Next step: use two labelled trays (today / altogether), model one example, then ask for an independent prediction on a different garden. Confidence: provisional; obtain a delayed check. This is a fictional example, not a learner assessment.

Send recommendations

Recommend educational adaptations for observed task demands, with educator/SENCO review. Use curated evidence-informed options such as explicit modelling, temporary scaffolds, metacognitive prompts, flexible grouping and accessible technology. Do not automatically diagnose, recommend statutory placement, change entitlement, or prescribe a clinical intervention.

Recommendation fields

  • Observed barrier and evidence

  • Alternative explanations

  • Proposed adaptation and rationale/source

  • When it may help and when it may not

  • Trial duration and owner

  • Outcome to check

  • Review/escalation route if concerns persist

Recommendation example

If multi-step instructions repeatedly interrupt otherwise successful reasoning: trial one instruction at a time with a visual sequence; compare independent performance on a matched task. Check reading, interface and sensory/motor demands before attributing the difficulty to SEND.

Privacy

Minimise child data; high-privacy defaults; separate private clinical/context notes from educational usage records. Define retention, deletion, export, access logs, vendor data use and contractual roles before launch. Obtain specialist review of the actual service and applicable requirements; consent is not a universal substitute for lawful processing.

Validation

  • Evidence-to-report factual accuracy

  • Unsupported/diagnostic claims must be blocked

  • Missing data produce explicit uncertainty

  • Independent educator ratings and correction rates

  • Accessibility and bias review across age, communication and access needs

  • Controlled release with human review; no silent reports sent to parents

Commercial cost implication

Tracking and assessment is a substantial product component, not a minor add-on to HTML pages. Begin with a minimal educator evidence record; add automation only when the evidence model and report quality are dependable.

Feedback

Check structured mathematical constraints when possible. AI can flag possible gaps and ask questions, but should not certify arbitrary free-form proofs as correct. Permit alternative valid approaches and expert review.

Assessment rubric

  • Correct statement and definitions

  • Appropriate assumptions/quantifiers

  • Logical validity of each inference

  • Completeness of cases

  • Non-circular structure

  • Clarity and connection to conclusion

Transfer

Assess later unaided writing on a different proposition; success with supplied tiles is not evidence of independent proof-writing ability.

Founder learning

Use the same exercises for personal proof practice, but maintain a more demanding track with external review and complete written proofs.

Looking together: the shared object

Can a screen become something people think with together?

A teacher, learner and shared representation form a useful lens for studying interaction. A screen may support shared exploration or divert attention from dialogue depending on task, placement, pace and use. Physical objects and paper can also support or disrupt shared attention.

Isserow’s 2008 art-therapy paper provides a conceptual reference about joint attention. Applying it to mathematics teaching is a hypothesis to investigate, not an established finding.

Research questions

  • When does an interactive representation invite shared attention and mathematical conversation?

  • When does individual screen use reduce opportunities for an educator to notice strategies or misconceptions?

  • Does automated feedback help learners revise their thinking, or encourage answer chasing?

  • How do teachers interpret automated performance summaries alongside direct observation?

  • How do learners describe the balance of screen, practical exploration and paper-based explanation?

Design implications

  • Shared viewing and turn-taking where useful

  • Pause points for prediction and explanation

  • A teacher can see the learner’s strategy, not only a score

  • Physical and paper alternatives as first-class parts of the task

  • Avoid equating gaze at a screen with disengagement; observe meaning and dialogue

  • Test individual and shared use rather than assuming one is universally better

Research gap status

No systematic search demonstrating a literature gap has been completed. Conduct a scoped review of joint attention, shared digital representations, automated feedback, teacher noticing and maths homework before claiming novelty.

A focused adult-educator interview study could explore experiences and generate hypotheses. It would not demonstrate effects on children or establish that screens cause disengagement.

Why mathematics when AI can do the work?

Position

AI challenges the balance of the curriculum and assessment, not the entire educational value of mathematics. The calculator analogy is useful but incomplete because AI can also generate explanations, code and purported proofs.

Reasons to teach

  • Quantitative agency in finance, risk, data and public claims

  • Modelling: selecting quantities, assumptions and relationships

  • Reasoning, proof and counterexample

  • Estimation and independent checks of tool outputs

  • Foundations for further technical study

  • Creative exploration and intrinsic interest

Limits

Do not claim that learning mathematics automatically improves every kind of reasoning, or that every traditional technique deserves unchanged emphasis.

Site implications

  • Predict before AI assistance

  • Inspect representations and explain why

  • Find errors and counterexamples in plausible solutions

  • Compare methods and state assumptions

  • Use tools for computation after developing enough understanding to judge results

  • Keep some independent fluency practice to make checking possible

Priorities and partnerships

  1. Stabilise a small flagship collection and define the evidence model.
  2. Commission master layouts, shared interaction patterns and independent mathematical review.
  3. Test usability and independent educator delivery.
  4. Pilot home-education workshops, school visits and the paid family/tutor offer.
  5. Build secure accounts and tracking only when the requirements are clear.
  6. Evaluate report quality, classroom use and retention before broad rollout.
  7. Develop educator training, creator partnerships and commissioning opportunities as evidence and delivery capacity grow.

Recruit good educators and evaluate their delivery. The materials should support a reliable baseline; no claim is made that teaching quality is irrelevant.

Future psychology expertise could inform design, consultation and evaluation. Professional qualification, service obligations and conflicts of interest must be handled separately from commercial product development.
