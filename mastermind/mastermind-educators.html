Maths Wednesdays · Educator notes

A good guess asks a good question.

Use Mastermind to practise interpreting evidence, eliminating possibilities and explaining a decision. Arithmetic is not the entry requirement.

Why use it?

Mastermind offers mathematical work with logic, constraints and systematic search. Deeper investigations lead to combinations, probability, information and algorithms.

Accessible entry is different from full analysis. A learner can explain why one code is impossible without being ready to design an optimal strategy. Adjust colours and holes, offer candidate cards, and value a justified deduction rather than speed.

A defensible description: “A short mathematical reasoning activity in which pupils use evidence, eliminate possibilities and justify their next move.”

“Warming up” is a useful informal description of engagement, not an established claim that playing improves general intelligence or performance in the next lesson. Research documents learning within Mastermind; broader transfer must be demonstrated rather than assumed. The sources below do not establish the immediate effect of a five-minute classroom starter.

A five-minute starter

  1. Show one unfinished position with a small number of possibilities.
  2. Give quiet thinking time: what must, might or cannot be true?
  3. Compare proposed moves in pairs. Ask: “What would this guess help us find out?”
  4. Choose a move together and explain what the new feedback rules out.

Keep the timer off for discussion. Completing a whole game is optional. A useful success criterion is: “I ruled out a possibility and explained why.”

For a practical version, use coloured counters and candidate-code cards. On screen, predict the remaining possibilities before revealing the count. Both formats should support conversation rather than replace it.

Investigation: should we keep a correct peg?

Suppose these are the only four codes remaining. Letters stand for distinct colours; repeats are allowed:

RAAA   RBBB   RCCC   RDDD

R is certainly first. Must our next guess keep it there? Can we invent a test whose feedback distinguishes all four codes?

Try first: make the four candidate cards. Propose a guess and sort the cards into groups that would give the same feedback. Compare the groups made by two different guesses.

Reveal one useful test

The deliberately impossible guess ABBC produces four different responses:

RAAA01
RBBB20
RCCC10
RDDD00

It cannot win immediately, but it identifies the code, so the next guess can win. By contrast, guessing RAAA either wins immediately or leaves three candidates with identical feedback.

This shows that replacing a known correct peg can help. It does not prove that doing so is always best.

Extend one idea at a time

  • Define “best”. Best chance of winning now, smallest worst-case group, or fewest guesses on average?
  • Test a rule. “Always use different colours” or “Always keep a correct peg.” Find a counterexample, then improve the rule.
  • Build a small strategy. Use two holes and three colours. List all codes and draw a branch for each possible response. Have we covered every case?
  • Explain an algorithm. Follow a rule, explain why it helps, identify its limits, then adapt it. Algorithms need not be learnt by rote.

Progression: try → predict → compare → justify → generalise. Counting possibilities or studying minimax can follow when the learner is ready.

Notice the reasoning

Look for use of earlier clues, distinctions between certainty and possibility, predictions before clicking, and willingness to revise a conjecture. Ask “How do you know?” and “Could another code fit?”

One lucky win or a fast score is not evidence of mastery. Averages are affected by settings, hints and strategy; time-outs and unfinished games also matter. Use a fresh position and an explanation to check understanding.
