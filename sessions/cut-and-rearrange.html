Maths Wednesdays · Shape & discovery

Cut. Slide. Turn.

Can the same pieces make a completely different shape? Make your cuts, then slide and turn every piece into the outline.

Move: drag a piece’s centre.Turn: select a piece, then drag a corner.Cut: drag from edge to edge.

What stays the same?

Each piece keeps its size and shape. Only its position and direction change.

Predict whether more cuts will make the rearrangement easier. Then compare the original, four-cut and five-cut examples.

Try it with paper

Trace the shape and its cut lines. Cut out the pieces and move them on the table. Can you explain where each corner ends up?

Cuts and pieces are counted separately: one cut can separate more than one existing piece.

Keyboard controls

In Move pieces mode, tab to a piece. Arrow keys move it; hold Shift for smaller movements. Q and E rotate it by 5°. Undo restores the previous move. During dragging, Shift snaps rotations to 15° steps.

For educators

The first construction is the exact Dudeney equilateral-triangle-to-square dissection, with four pieces. Additional cut examples subdivide these pieces, preserving the same solution. These variants are not claims about minimum numbers of cuts. All automatic moves preserve area and use translations and rotations, without reflections or resizing.

Learners can make their own straight cuts. Automatic arrangement is available when their pieces refine the demonstrated construction; it is not a general dissection solver. Precut examples always have a verified arrangement. Small alignment errors snap to the example’s target positions. Alternative correct tilings are accepted by the area and overlap check.

Begin with the long rectangle or parallelogram. The equilateral triangle is an extension. Distinguish cutting from folding, area from perimeter, and number of cut segments from number of resulting pieces.

Inspired by Numberphile: Squaring the Triangle. Construction reference: Greg N. Frederickson, Hinged Dissections. Original interactive implementation.
