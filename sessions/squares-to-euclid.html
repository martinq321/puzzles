The divisions so far

Repeated equal square removals combine into one division.

    Why does this find a common factor?

    Suppose a square size divides both sides of a rectangle. Removing a whole square leaves a remainder whose sides are still multiples of that size. The possible common factors stay the same at each step.

    At the end, the last square’s side is the highest common factor (HCF), also called the greatest common divisor (GCD). It is the biggest whole-number square size that tiles the original rectangle using only equal squares.

    The repeated division is the Euclidean algorithm.

    This method finds common factors. It does not always use the fewest squares. Compare with Fewest Squares.

    For educators · a natural progression

    For repeated square removal, 18×12 gives a clear starting example: 18 = 1×12 + 6, then 12 = 2×6 + 0. HCF = 6. Use the equal-squares overlay to connect the final side length back to the original rectangle.

    13×8 ends in unit squares: its sides are coprime. Let learners cut paper or mark the same steps before introducing the algorithm’s name. Square removal here is a deterministic method, not an optimisation solver.
