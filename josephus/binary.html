Why does moving one bit work?

Explore this after trying the cards. We number positions from 1, keep position 1 and remove position 2, then continue round the remaining cards.

Gold marks the original leading 1. Place values are shown below the bits. Leading zeros after the move do not change the value.

1. Find the extra cards

Call the largest power of two no greater than N P. Call the extra amount L. Thus N = P + L, with 0 ≤ L < P.

When the total is a power of two, the first card survives: one round removes every second card, leaving half as many, with the same first card. Repeat until only that card remains.

2. Reduce to a power of two

Show the remaining order for this example

This is 2L + 1, where L is the extra amount. It is not 2N + 1, where N is the total number of cards.

3. Connect this to the binary digits

Removing the leading 1 subtracts P and leaves L. Appending a 0 would double L; appending a 1 doubles it and adds 1. So moving that leading 1 to the end produces exactly 2L + 1.

Try, predict, then check

Use 7, 8, 9, then 15, 16, 17 cards. What happens just after a power of two? Try 12 and 13: what changes in the final binary digit pattern?

Further explanation: even and odd totals

Let J(N) mean the last position. For N = 2m, the first pass removes all even positions. The remaining positions 1, 3, …, 2m−1 relabel as 1, 2, …, m. Therefore J(2m) = 2J(m) − 1.

For N = 2m+1 with m ≥ 1, remove the even positions, keep 2m+1 and remove 1. Counting restarts at 3. The remaining positions 3, 5, …, 2m+1 relabel as 1, 2, …, m. Therefore J(2m+1) = 2J(m) + 1. The base case is J(1) = 1.

These rules use a fixed starting position and remove every second card. Changing either rule changes the answer; the binary rotation is not a formula for every counting-out game.
